## Main finding

These samples support the user's impression: ordinary Steambot conversation often uses simpler camera coverage than the supplied Genshin sequence. Several speakers and several turns can share a stable composition. Head turns, body orientation, brief gestures, and the choice of who remains visible carry much of the interaction.

The camera is not completely fixed. The clips include closer reactions, reverses, gradual changes of framing, and elaborate reveal shots. The wreckage scene particularly prevents a blanket claim that all Steambot staging is simple. Its event sequence is more elaborate than its subsequent conversation.

For IMC, the most promising baseline is a readable shared view, with selective changes when attention or relationships change. This is a creative recommendation based on the samples, not evidence about either game's internal tools or production costs.

## 1. Deck conversation: shared framing makes humor social

### Observed sequence

- **00:00–00:08:** A gameplay view and fade lead into Connie and Vanilla approaching the group. Establishing who is present precedes the exchange.
- **00:09–00:16:** A loose group composition accommodates greetings and the question about their delay. Around 00:13, the yellow-shirted man raises a hand; by approximately 00:14, it has lowered while the line remains on screen. The gesture can finish before the player advances.
- **00:17–00:22:** A choice offers excuses or rumors. After the rumor choice, Vanilla makes a brief hand/arm gesture while suggesting Connie wanted more time alone with him. The group stays visible, and his arm returns toward rest.
- **00:24–00:28:** Connie turns toward Vanilla and protests. Framing moves closer to her response, retaining another person in the composition. Her embarrassment is given emphasis within the social situation.
- **00:29–00:36:** The wider group arrangement returns. Another person questions Connie, and she protests again. The response to Vanilla's teasing spreads through the group.
- **00:39–00:51:** Conversation returns to lyrics and practice. Multiple turns remain in shared framing. A parenthesized interface instruction explains how to perform the music without assigning that instruction to a character's ordinary speech.
- **00:54–00:57:** Attention shifts to people on the upper deck. A different view has a concrete new subject.

### Why this works

The joke has an audience. Keeping several people visible lets the player understand why Connie reacts and why another person joins in. The shared view preserves the social consequence of the player's choice.

Vanilla also participates physically. Selecting an attitude is followed by a spoken response and an action; he is not just an invisible menu operator.

The closer reaction is selective. It follows a change in the social situation rather than every change of speaker. The wider composition then supports ordinary conversation again.

### Possible IMC use

An officer-group exchange can stay in a shared view while someone speaks, the person addressed turns toward them, and a third person registers the exchange. The precise reaction must come from the character and situation. Do not make every listener smile, every joke produce a gesture, or a reserved character suddenly become demonstrative.

For player choices, plan the selected line, its recipient's response, and the return to a stable pose. A branch does not automatically require its own camera setup.

## 2. Wreckage and Savory: elaborate events, economical conversation

### Observed sequence

- **00:00–00:06:** The damaged vessel establishes the aftermath. A closer view shows Connie physically supporting the man.
- **00:08–00:12:** A wider view shows him on the ground with the others nearby. The physical situation has changed, so the wider frame is informative.
- **00:14–00:22:** He is collapsed over the wreckage; closer framing emphasizes his despair and fear of his superior.
- **00:24–00:34:** The group occupies the foreground, looking toward him. The same arrangement accommodates a player choice, Vanilla's reply, and the man's frightened change of posture.
- **00:36–00:40:** A low view places the purple machine prominently behind/above him. Relative scale and position establish the new threat.
- **00:42–00:56:** The sampled framing progresses from Savory's lower body to her face, followed by a distinctive head/hair movement. This is a deliberately staged introduction with substantially more visual activity than the everyday exchanges.
- **Approximately 00:58–01:30:** The confrontation settles into a wide arrangement: the party on one side, the man between them and Savory, and the machine behind her. Several lines and reactions use that shared geography.
- **Approximately 01:35–01:58:** Closer coverage returns to Vanilla and Connie as their immediate response becomes the focus. Their choice of what to do next is visually separated from the opposing group's introduction.

### Why this works

The physical event and the conversation have different needs. Supporting someone, discovering a threat, and introducing Savory each need the player to see new information. Once everyone is established, the wide confrontation view can explain their relationship for several exchanges.

The machine's scale and placement do some of the storytelling. The scene does not rely solely on a close face shot to communicate power.

### Possible IMC use

Reserve custom entrances, discoveries, object transfers, major relocations, and other elaborate staging for events that need them. Routine officer dialogue can return to a simpler shared setup afterward.

Do not use the quieter nighttime scene as evidence that a dramatic entrance can always be produced with equally little animation. These clips show a range of staging complexity.

## 3. Nighttime conversation: emotional turns within an ordinary pose

### Observed sequence

- **00:00–00:20:** Player thought/menu material precedes the main exchange. Its camera and UI should not be treated as uninterrupted cinematic conversation.
- **Approximately 00:23–00:32:** Connie approaches the seated Vanilla. The scene moves into both sitting beside one another on the low stone edge.
- **00:35–00:47:** Shared side framing shows them looking outward/upward as the conversation opens. Their seated bodies and hands remain relatively settled while the composition changes gently.
- **00:50–01:26:** A rear two-person view accommodates permission to ask questions, food preferences, multiple choices, and Connie's speculation about Vanilla's origins. Speaker changes do not require alternating isolated close-ups.
- **01:29–01:59:** Another angle emphasizes the continuing exchange and Vanilla's response. The choice to let Connie continue, decline, or ask about her can wait within a stable seated setup.
- **02:02–02:14:** A personal choice leads to Vanilla asking about boys she likes. Connie turns her attention outward/away during her hesitant answer. The physical deflection is legible while both remain together.
- **02:17–02:29:** Connie notices the pendant and gestures toward Vanilla's upper chest. Framing comes closer with her in the foreground, and Vanilla looks down. Recognition gives the movement and reframing a shared cause.
- **02:32–03:14:** The scene returns to a shared front/side view. Connie's lowered head and gaze persist while the exchange covers Mallow and related questions. Around 03:14, her restrained description of him as a friend is accompanied by this posture.
- **03:17–03:23:** Connie looks upward/outward, then turns toward Vanilla. Changes of gaze remain visible within the same general composition.
- **03:26–03:38:** She redirects them toward playing music together. The same seated arrangement supports the new activity's invitation and instrument choices.
- **03:39 onward:** A visible save-state load returns to an earlier choice. The subsequently repeated pendant discovery must not be interpreted as the scene naturally circling back after the music invitation.

### Why this works

Sitting beside someone provides a believable alternative to maintaining eye contact through every line. A glance toward the other person, away toward the water, or down at one's hands can carry different meanings without requiring a new position.

Connie's lowered gaze while discussing Mallow is an observable action. Reading it as guarded attachment or difficulty speaking is interpretation supported by the surrounding subtitles; the visual alone cannot establish her entire inner state.

The move to shared music can be read as a way to continue spending time together after a difficult subject. The scene permits the feeling to remain unresolved rather than requiring an explanatory confession.

The pendant is especially useful evidence: an object becomes important because someone notices it. The notice, gesture, Vanilla's downward look, and closer framing all relate to the same discovery. They are not four unrelated embellishments.

### Possible IMC use

Companion scenes can begin from resting, watching something together, waiting, or sitting nearby. Characters do not always need to keep working, handle props, or face one another squarely to look alive.

This suits the user's established conversational mechanisms: ordinary shared time, a personal question, partial disclosure, a listener's response, then a practical way to remain together. Borrow the mechanism without giving IMC characters Connie's exact behavior or assuming the same romantic relationship.

The same principle applies to contained professional characters. A change in attention can be sufficient when it is visible and meaningful. Avoid prescribing a stereotyped gesture as a permanent personality marker.

## Comparison with the supplied Genshin sequence

These are tendencies in the inspected samples, not universal rules for either game.

| Aspect | Genshin sample | Steambot samples | IMC implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| Conversational coverage | Frequent individual/shoulder-focused framing alongside shared views | Extended group or two-person compositions across several turns | Establish a useful shared view that can carry a complete exchange |
| Directing attention | Selected closer views, listener coverage, expressive poses | Facing changes, body orientation, brief gestures, selective reframing | The listener can become important without a mandatory cut |
| Quiet emotion | Close reactions and changes in engagement | Seated posture and gaze remain readable within shared space | Allow stillness and small attention shifts |
| Humor | Character performance and selected reaction emphasis | The visible audience and group response strengthen the joke | Keep relevant witnesses visible where their reaction matters |
| Dramatic events | Special framing for reveals, subjects, and locations | Wreckage scene has explicit physical action and staged reveal | Keep major-event staging available without making it the routine baseline |
| Player choice | Decision framing and held poses | Choices often remain inside an established composition | Design a stable waiting state and an action that can finish |

Neither reference supports a rigid rule such as one new angle per speaker, one gesture per line, or a fixed number of seconds per shot. A recorded choice hold partly measures the player's reading and decision time.

## Proposed IMC direction to consider

### Routine company conversation

Start with the relevant people and their spatial relationship clearly visible. Let that setup cover several connected thoughts. Use brief physical responses where a line changes someone's attention or behavior. Reframe when the current view can no longer show something important.

### Quiet companion conversation

Choose a credible shared situation and comfortable default posture. Allow looking away and listening. Use closer emphasis sparingly, when an existing shared view cannot communicate a meaningful detail. Leave room for an emotional subject to end through a change of activity rather than a speech explaining it.

### Major story events

Add bespoke actions or shots for the entrance, reveal, conflict, departure, or discovery that warrants them. Return to a simpler composition when the event gives way to conversation.

These are practical working categories, not engine modes, mandatory camera counts, or approved project standards. Whether they actually reduce cost depends on IMC's art, rigs, animation library, dialogue system, and supported camera views. A sprite-based implementation may express the same intent through orientation, spacing, poses, and modest reframing rather than reproducing a 3D orbit or facial close-up.

## How a later manuscript pass could stay readable

If approved, define the scene's starting arrangement once, then annotate meaningful changes. Avoid adding technical instructions between every line or fragmenting a naturally unfolding thought to make room for camera cuts.

At a meaningful beat, the useful questions are:

1. What line, object, or event causes the response?
2. Whose attention or posture changes?
3. Can the existing shared view show it clearly?
4. What pose or arrangement can safely wait for player input afterward?

The principle is the same as the dialogue skill's cause-and-response approach. Physical actions should answer the conversation. A character need not visibly perform their profile on every turn.

Before a Scene 3 revision, read its current manuscript and current character sheets again. Preview the complete proposed scene with staging and wait for approval before editing. This research does not authorize rewriting dialogue, changing character identities, or making either reference game's direction mandatory.

## Working conclusion

Steambot's shared-view conversation is the user-selected reference for IMC's dialogue staging, including ordinary conversation, quiet companionship, and selectively elaborate story events. Do not blend it with Genshin.
