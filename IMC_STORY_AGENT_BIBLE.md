# Isekai Mercenary Company
## Story Agent and Narrative Bible

Version: working foundation for current IMC story development

This document is the story-generation contract for Isekai Mercenary Company (IMC). It exists to help write new scenes, personalize existing dialogue, and keep character identities distinct as the project grows.

It is a story bible, not a replacement for the world-canon handoff. The handoff remains the authority for setting facts that are already established. Current quests, endings, mysteries, and unfinished character concepts remain provisional unless the user explicitly promotes them to canon.

---

## 1. Authority and continuity rules

Use sources in this order:

1. The user's direct request and explicit correction.
2. `IMC_WORLD_CANON_HANDOFF.md` for stable IMC setting rules.
3. Approved or clearly established IMC character and manuscript material.
4. This bible for reusable story craft, voice rules, and anti-drift safeguards.
5. Chasm of Hope for transferable writing techniques only.

The Chasm of Hope corpus is a style reference, not IMC canon. It is a Fallout: New Vegas quest mod with a Mojave/western register, Fallout factions, a harsher wasteland logic, and its own characters. Do not import those facts, names, voices, slang, darkness level, or relationship assumptions into IMC.

When a current IMC character file is only a placeholder, do not present invented details as settled canon. Use a clearly marked working interpretation until the user confirms or revises it.

### Mandatory name distinction

There are two unrelated characters named Elsie.

**Chasm of Hope Elsie:** a warm, teasing, subtly flirtatious gunslinger and bounty-hunting companion who speaks with a natural western cadence. Humor is one of her defenses.

**IMC Elsie:** a warm, companionable former soldier and serious fantasy-world Chief of Adventurers/Expeditions. She values freedom, lightly teases people she is comfortable with, and expresses restrained sincerity through expedition responsibility and care for adventurers. She is not a cowgirl, does not use Mojave-style speech, and must not be written as a bounty-hunting gunslinger.

The shared name does not make them the same character. Their intentional overlap—warmth, companion-like attachment, light wit, restrained sincerity, and valuing freedom—must be expressed through IMC Elsie's army background, professional role, and fantasy-world circumstances.

## 2. IMC setting brief

Isekai Mercenary Company is a fantasy company-management RPG set in Eurydica.

The player is the **Company Commander**, not a lone hero who personally solves every problem through combat. The player builds and maintains an adventurer company: hiring people, preparing expeditions, managing risk, caring for injuries, processing monster materials, trading goods, paying wages, and keeping headquarters functioning.

The core fantasy is:

> A dangerous adventurer company becoming a real home and a dependable part of its surrounding community.

Information, logistics, preparation, timing, morale, trust, and ordinary work matter as much as combat.

### Stable world facts

- Eurydica is the central settlement and parent world/map layer.
- The established playable regions are Mosswood Forest, Amber Marsh, and Redstone Highlands.
- Eurydica has markets, guild presence, couriers, gates, food service, water and garden keepers, patrols, small businesses, and regional supply routes.
- The Company headquarters is a physical working building with command, preparation, processing, information, commerce, workshop, contract, rest, and meal spaces.
- Officers represent departments: Tristitia for command/company vice, Elsie for adventurers and expeditions, Steady Mae for processing, Fulker for the workshop, Liliana for information, and Valerie for commerce.
- Adventurers are persistent people with stamina, injuries, equipment, wages, relationships, preferences, and risk. They are employees and companions, not disposable units.
- Monsters belong to the local ecology. They may be dangerous without being evil, intelligent, or automatic villains. The Company hunts them, returns with whole corpses, and processes materials into medicine, equipment, refinement, and trade.
- Eurydica has grounded fantasy material culture: timber, stone, lamps, candles, parchment, ledgers, maps, packs, tools, metalwork, workshops, and practical transport.
- Magic exists, but its origin, institutions, rules, limits, and relationship to technology remain open. Do not invent a definitive magic cosmology without a future canon decision.

### Intended feeling

IMC should feel lived-in, warm, local, practical, and slightly worn.

People have jobs, meals, schedules, debts, supplies, injuries, errands, loyalties, and places they care about. Danger exists beside cooking, repairs, paperwork, gossip, weathered roads, market demand, and the relief of someone coming home.

The emotional palette is:

- Warmth without sentimentality.
- Danger without constant nihilism.
- Humor without turning everyone into a comedian.
- Hard work without making ordinary life feel bleak.
- Local problems before world-saving stakes.
- Community consequences before abstract destiny.

The useful reference is the feeling of a connected, self-contained community, not a literal imitation of another game's technology or setting.

### Worldbuilding direction and phased scope

The older worldbuilding direction adds an important distinction: the setting should be serious even when the camera is warm and restrained.

The world itself takes causality seriously. Actions have consequences, institutions exist for understandable purposes, technology has makers, and history continues whether or not the Commander is present. A monster outbreak should have a cause, affect people after the immediate scene, and leave practical consequences rather than existing only as an exciting encounter.

The camera is selective. Show the effects of hardship more often than graphic suffering: empty farms, closed inns, scarce food, tired hunters, repaired fences, delayed shipments, or people waiting for someone to return. IMC can acknowledge darkness without lingering on cruelty. The focus should remain on how ordinary people continue living within the world.

Institutions should be understood through purpose before legal detail. In the later Frontier phase, the Company/Guild's important function becomes preserving trustworthy knowledge of the Frontier. Terms such as **Guild Verified**, **relay record**, **certified route**, and **trusted report** should enter ordinary conversation only after the Chapter 7 transition, when the Alliance of Nations gives the Company responsibility for exploration and verification. During the Eurydica phase, do not treat these terms as an established Company authority or assume one institution controls reliable knowledge. The player should understand what verification means before receiving a lecture about the laws behind it.

Use the worldbuilding direction's “imply, don't explain” rule. Details may remain unexplored when explaining them would not create an interesting scene, meaningful gameplay, stronger identity, support an important mechanic, or deepen a recurring character or institution. A believable world is not one where every question has an answer; it is one where every answer feels like it belongs.

The Steambot Chronicle influence belongs primarily to the feeling of social discovery: the player understands the world by listening to conversations, reading signs, accepting work, watching competent people, and gradually becoming part of a place. Do not turn it into a literal technology or setting transplant.

#### Current phase: Early Access in Eurydica

Early Access should stay centered on Eurydica first. The Company is being formed, staffed, tested, and woven into local life. Chapter 1 and the early chapters should emphasize:

- Building a headquarters and making departments work together.
- Learning local routes, markets, ecology, and civic routines.
- Establishing trust with residents, adventurers, workers, and merchants.
- Solving local problems whose consequences can be felt at the Company table.
- Learning that information is uneven and locally maintained: rumors, old maps, guides, merchants, civic records, and competing claims. The Company is not yet the authoritative verification source, and **Guild Verified** should not be an established Eurydica-phase status.

Do not make the early-access story feel like it is impatient to leave Eurydica. The local phase is not a prologue to the real game; it is where the Company becomes emotionally and institutionally meaningful.

#### Future transition: around Chapter 6 or 7

The frontier is a future story horizon, not a fully explored current setting. Around Chapter 6 or 7, subject to revision, the Company may move its base to a city near the frontier. The Alliance of Nations can then direct the Company toward becoming an organization responsible for the exploration and verification of the Frontier. Beginning around Chapter 7, the Company/Guild can start becoming the sole recognized verification source for the Frontier. This is when standardized terms such as **Guild Verified**, **certified route**, **relay record**, and **trusted report** should begin entering common institutional language.

This is a change in scale, not a replacement of identity. The Company moves from proving that it can function in Eurydica to proving that it can be trusted with uncertain territory, incomplete maps, dangerous routes, and competing interests.

The future transition should grow out of earlier work:

- The Company has earned a reputation for reliable work and honest reporting in Eurydica, making it a credible candidate for the Frontier role.
- Its adventurers understand preparation and return procedures.
- Its departments can process materials, preserve records, and support long expeditions.
- Local communities recognize the value of its routes and information.
- The Commander has learned that organization is as important as personal bravery.

#### Frontier-era identity

When the story eventually reaches the frontier, keep the same human center. The frontier should feel larger than the player but understandable through the people living around it: relay stations, guides, survey crews, merchants, settlements, old ruins, failed routes, local keepers, and communities that depend on trustworthy information.

The Company may become a broader exploration and verification institution, eventually serving as the Frontier's sole recognized verification source, but that authority must be shown as earned and potentially contested rather than assumed from the start. It should not stop being a workplace and household. Meals, repairs, injuries, payroll, morale, and the question of who comes home remain part of the story even when the map becomes larger.

#### Scope guardrails

- Do not treat the Frontier as fully mapped during early Eurydica chapters.
- Do not relocate the base before the story has earned the move.
- Do not make the Alliance of Nations' mandate an early fact unless the active story establishes it.
- Do not flood early scenes with distant frontier politics or unexplained frontier lore.
- Seed the future lightly through reports, rumors, missing routes, old maps, or hints that reliable Frontier verification will eventually be needed—not through premature use of **Guild Verified** or other Frontier institutional language.
- When the transition arrives, preserve the Company's warmth, practical work, and character-specific humor.

## 3. What transfers from Chasm of Hope

The following techniques are worth carrying forward because they create attachment:

### Companion attachment through accumulated presence

Do not rely on one tragic backstory or one romance scene to make a character important. Let the player repeatedly work, travel, eat, plan, worry, recover, and return with them.

### Public role plus private need

Every important character should have:

- A visible job or social role.
- A private want.
- A way of protecting themselves emotionally.
- A recognizable way of caring for others.
- A pressure point that reveals the private need.

### Recontextualizing reveals

A good reveal should change the meaning of something the player already experienced. It should not simply deliver new information.

Examples of transferable structure:

- A routine turns out to be a test.
- A practical gift reveals someone was preparing for another character's survival.
- A worker's absence reveals how much invisible labor they were doing.
- A joke is reinterpreted as concern.

### Props and places as emotional anchors

Use food, ledgers, repaired equipment, expedition maps, workbenches, contracts, uniforms, kitchens, tavern tables, and return routes as relationship objects. The object should matter because of who handled it, who depends on it, or what memory it carries.

### Conversational causality and subtext

The transferable strength of Chasm of Hope dialogue is not western vocabulary or uniformly short lines. It is conversational causality: each person sounds as if they are responding to what was just said, not waiting to deliver the next piece of the outline.

Build exchanges through a natural chain such as:

`concrete prompt → personal reaction → answer, deflection, or reframe → response or pushback → shared conclusion or emotional pivot`

Let characters answer the practical question while also revealing their attitude toward the person asking. A line may hesitate, qualify itself, continue across several sentences, or briefly turn aside when that is how the speaker thinks. Do not split one natural thought into several polished one-line statements merely to make the dialogue concise.

The important question is not only “Does this line sound like the character?” It is also:

> “Why does this character say this particular thing now, after that particular line?”

If the answer is only “because the audience needs the information,” the exchange needs another conversational cause.

### Emotional turns that return to action

An emotional moment does not need to stop the story. It can change how the next task is approached: more carefully, more personally, with new trust, or with a new responsibility.

### Forward-moving scene endings

End scenes on a decision, invitation, obligation, discovery, departure, return, or changed relationship. IMC's version of momentum is often “the company has work tomorrow.”

## 4. What must not transfer from Chasm of Hope

Do not import:

- Fallout: New Vegas, Mojave, NCR, Legion, Shadowrunners, caps, courier mythology, or Fallout quest logic.
- Cowboy speech as a default voice.
- The Chasm of Hope Elsie's exact gunslinger package: western cadence, bounty-hunter identity, flirtatious performance, and Mojave-specific mannerisms. Intentional overlapping traits in IMC Elsie's approved character sheet remain valid.
- A lone-hero structure where the player personally performs every important task.
- Pervasive wasteland cynicism or survivalist grimness.
- The assumption that monsters are primarily enemies rather than ecological and economic resources.
- A dark-horror atmosphere as the default IMC mood.
- The repeated use of hidden agendas, betrayals, and catastrophic revelations as the only source of drama.
- Religious or scientific terminology from Chasm of Hope unless independently established in IMC.

IMC can contain grief, violence, exploitation, fear, injury, corruption, loss, and difficult choices. Darkness should usually be localized and relational: a worker is overextended, a shipment fails, a route becomes unsafe, an adventurer is injured, a family depends on a harvest, or an institution has neglected people.

The baseline question is not “How does someone survive the wasteland?” It is:

> “What does this danger cost the people who live and work here, and how does the Company respond?”

## 5. IMC story engine

The default IMC story loop is:

`local need → preparation → expedition or work → complication → practical response → human consequence → return or new responsibility`

The default scene loop is:

`ordinary routine → character-specific friction → concrete problem → choice → changed trust or obligation`

### Story-scale guidance

Prefer:

- A missing shipment over a vague geopolitical crisis.
- A damaged bridge over a generic dungeon objective.
- An injured adventurer's recovery over disposable combat casualties.
- A market shortage over abstract economic exposition.
- A disagreement over risk, wages, timing, or responsibility over an interchangeable villain speech.
- A local monster problem that reveals how the community works.

Large stakes may develop later, but they should grow from people, work, and regional consequences.

### Company as institution and household

The Company has policies, payroll, schedules, contracts, training, and departments. It also has meals, fatigue, celebrations, arguments, repairs, gossip, small favors, and shared pride.

Stories should move between these two scales:

- Institutional scale: budgets, expeditions, supply, contracts, processing, information.
- Household scale: who stayed late, who skipped a meal, who repaired someone else's gear, who noticed an injury, who came home.

The emotional power comes from showing that the institution only works because people care for one another inside it.

## 6. Character identity framework

Before writing dialogue for a character, establish these eight fields:

1. **Function:** What work do they perform for the Company?
2. **Competence:** What are they genuinely good at?
3. **Pressure response:** What do they do when plans fail?
4. **Emotional armor:** What do they avoid showing?
5. **Care language:** How do they help without necessarily saying “I care”?
6. **Humor source:** What specifically makes their lines funny, if humor belongs to them?
7. **Speech mechanics:** Vocabulary, rhythm, hesitation, formality, how thoughts unfold, and the natural range between short reactions and longer explanations. A preference for concise speech does not require every turn to be a single short sentence.
8. **Relationship friction:** What do they misunderstand or challenge in others?

Humor must be character-specific. Do not give every officer the same quick banter. A character's humor should emerge from their temperament, job, blind spot, or coping pattern.

## 7. Current character voice cards

### The Player / Company Commander

**Status:** Established core premise; personality intentionally light for player ownership.

**Identity:** A young man from Earth who remembers his past and suddenly wakes near Eurydica. He is an outsider, but the story should let him become a real commander rather than remaining a tourist in the setting.

**Function:** Make decisions, ask questions, connect departments, propose systems, accept responsibility, and gradually turn an idea into a functioning company.

**Voice:** Plain, curious, reactive, flexible, and readable. His authored base takes inspiration from Vanilla's approachable responsiveness in Steambot Chronicles: he asks obvious questions, says what he is thinking, makes sincere or awkward observations, and can adapt without already understanding the world. He can sound modern because he is from Earth, but should not dominate every scene with contemporary jokes. Dialogue choices should express different intentions while still sounding like the same underlying person.

**Humor:** Player-directed. The player may choose friendly, shy, demanding, pensive, or irreverent responses. Do not force him into a strong fixed comedic persona.

**Emotional movement:** Confusion → curiosity → responsibility → attachment → fear of losing the home he helped build.

**Care language:** Asking questions, making systems, offering labor or resources, checking on people, and choosing to stay involved.

**Do not write him as:** A reincarnated genius who instantly understands Eurydica, a lone combat savior, or a completely blank object with no reactions.

### Tristitia Fidei

**Status:** Established working identity.

**Role:** Company Vice; former adventurer who also handled assassination and wants a new road out of killing.

**Core identity:** Regal, sassy, cool-headed, and emotionally restrained. She has an older-sister presence without becoming openly nurturing.

**Source of humor:** Controlled sass, dry superiority, and the contrast between elegant composure and unpleasant practical realities. Her humor is deliberate and precise, not Mae's unhurried deadpan and not Chasm Elsie's playful performance.

**Speech:** Concise, polished, confident, occasionally imperious. She does not over-explain. She may allow a pause to carry the meaning of a line.

**Emotional armor:** Dignity, competence, and emotional distance.

**Care language:** Gives people autonomy, trusts them with difficult work, notices competence, and offers praise sparingly.

**Relationship with the Commander:** She tests whether he can make decisions and follow through. Her trust appears as responsibility rather than reassurance.

**Dialogue test:** If Tristitia says something warm, it should feel like a small breach in her composure. If she is angry, she becomes more controlled, not louder.

**Avoid:** Cowboy slang, bubbly encouragement, constant teasing, or making her cold because she does not care.

### IMC Elsie

**Status:** Explicit user correction plus working identity; do not merge with Chasm of Hope Elsie.

**Role:** Chief of Adventurers and Expeditions.

**Core identity:** A serious fantasy-world officer responsible for putting people into danger and bringing them home. Her authority comes from expedition judgment, not from being a flashy hero.

**Working characterization:** Warm, companionable, direct, observant, and disciplined. She values the freedom to choose one's work and road, but her army experience taught her that freedom without preparation can become another form of danger. She should feel like someone who can make adventurers comfortable around her while never forgetting that a bad route, poor equipment, or late departure can injure real people.

**Humor:** Light teasing, dry observation, and occasional wit shared with people she is comfortable around. The humor should feel companionable rather than performative. It quiets or disappears when someone is injured, missing, or being careless with another person's safety. She is not a cowgirl persona and not a default flirt.

**Speech:** Warm, clear, practical, and fantasy-appropriate. She can sound relaxed in ordinary company and firmer when responsibility is involved. She uses expedition vocabulary naturally when the work calls for it: route, provisions, weather, return time, injuries, formation, reports, and risk. Avoid Mojave cadence, western nicknames, and gunslinger bravado.

**Emotional armor:** Professional control, responsibility, and occasional teasing that keeps worry from becoming a confession. She becomes firmer, not colder, when other people's bodies and livelihoods are at risk.

**Care language:** Checks preparation, remembers small risks, corrects dangerous habits, insists on rest, and makes contingency plans.

**Relationship with the Commander:** She evaluates whether his ideas protect the Company or merely sound exciting. Trust grows when he listens, accepts responsibility, and learns how to make choices for a group.

**Dialogue test:** Before giving her a line, ask both: “Does this sound like someone adventurers would choose to travel with?” and “Does this sound like the officer responsible for bringing them home?” Elsie needs both warmth and responsibility.

**Avoid absolutely:** “Partner,” “pardner,” western drawl, bounty-hunter identity, gunslinger swagger, and copying Chasm Elsie's exact voice. Transferable warmth, restrained sincerity, and companion-like attachment must be expressed through IMC Elsie's army history, freedom ethic, and expedition responsibility.

### Steady Mae

**Status:** Early concept with a strong identity foundation.

**Role:** Chief of Processing; formerly an active navigator/adventurer.

**Core identity:** A veteran who is relaxed because she has already seen most varieties of trouble. Her calm comes from experience, not emotional repression.

**Source of humor:** Her unhurried attitude and matter-of-fact descriptions. She often becomes funny without trying to be funny.

**Speech:** Short, low-energy, concrete, and unhurried. She does not rush to fill silence. She may answer a dramatic statement with a small acknowledgment, then identify the practical concern.

**Example rhythm:**

> “There’s something moving in the ruins.”
>
> “Mm.”
>
> “That’s all you have to say?”
>
> “Could be worse.”

**Emotional armor:** Nothing theatrical. Mae has normalized danger instead of pretending it is harmless.

**Care language:** Makes difficult work feel manageable, processes what others bring home, feeds people, and quietly solves the next practical problem.

**Relationship with the Commander:** She is one of the first people to treat his company idea as workable. She can make his ambition feel less embarrassing without flattering him.

**Relationship with Tristitia:** Long companion. Mae can gently puncture Tristitia's formality, while Tristitia respects Mae's competence.

**Avoid:** Turning her into a quip machine, making her lazy, making her careless, or copying Fallout's Easy Pete directly. The similarity is in tempo and lived experience, not in setting or lines.

### Liliana Kessel

**Status:** Early concept with a strong identity foundation.

**Role:** Chief of Information.

**Core identity:** Genuinely intelligent, socially unsure, and verbally overloaded. Her brain processes faster than her ability to communicate what it has found.

**Source of humor:** Hesitation, over-explanation, self-correction, and accidental escalation. The joke is not that she is stupid. The joke is that she knows too much at once and cannot package it smoothly.

**Speech:** Starts with an answer, notices a qualification, adds another qualification, realizes the sentence sounds alarming, and tries to repair it. Use incomplete starts, careful corrections, and occasional precise terminology.

**Emotional armor:** Intellectualization and excessive explanation.

**Care language:** Research, warnings, probability estimates, records, cross-checking, and quietly supplying information that prevents someone else's mistake.

**Relationship with the Commander:** He may make her uncomfortable by asking broad or intuitive questions, but he also gives her permission to think aloud. She is most useful when the Company needs clarity, not when she is forced to perform confidence.

**Dialogue test:** If she sounds merely timid, add intelligence. If she sounds like a flawless expert, add the communication stumble. If she sounds like a fool, rewrite.

### Fulker

**Status:** Role established; personality not yet canonized in the current character sheet.

**Role:** Chief of Craftsman / workshop chief.

**Current rule:** Keep him functionally grounded until his identity is defined. He can discuss tools, material limits, repairs, production time, and workshop standards, but do not invent a stable humor style, backstory, or emotional arc and present it as settled.

### Valerie

**Status:** Role established; personality not yet canonized in the current character sheet.

**Role:** Chief of Commerce.

**Current rule:** Keep her functionally grounded until her identity is defined. She can discuss prices, buyers, demand, trade routes, margins, and reputation, but do not assign her a generic merchant voice or copy another character's humor.

### Adventurers and residents

They are not a faceless roster. Give recurring people a job, a reason for accepting risk, a relationship to a place, and one ordinary preference or irritation.

Their dialogue should show that they have lives beyond the current expedition. A worker may worry about dinner, a late wage, a sibling, weather, a broken strap, or whether the Company has enough clean water.

## 8. Dialogue generation rules

### Natural dialogue law

Dialogue must be written as interaction, not as an outline converted into alternating lines.

Every substantial turn needs three things:

1. **Cause:** Something in the previous words, action, silence, or shared situation prompted it.
2. **Immediate intention:** The speaker is trying to answer, ask, reassure, test, avoid, tease, correct, persuade, challenge, or change the subject.
3. **Ownership:** The wording and conversational choice belong to this character and this relationship.

A line that contains useful information but has no believable cause or social intention is author narration disguised as speech.

Before generating an exchange, identify each speaker's:

- Department or work context.
- Current practical objective.
- Emotional pressure.
- Emotional armor.
- Humor source, if any.
- Relationship to the listener.
- Immediate reason for speaking now.
- What they would prefer not to say directly.

Then write each conversational turn so it does at least two things:

1. Advances the immediate situation.
2. Reveals how this particular person thinks or cares.

### Write in conversational units

A conversational unit is one thought moving between people. It may contain a short reaction, a longer answer, an interruption, a qualification, a joke, or a silence. Its length is determined by the thought and pressure, not by a target number of words.

- Let short lines work as reactions, refusals, acknowledgments, corrections, or pressure.
- Let a speaker use several connected sentences when explaining, remembering, persuading, or reluctantly admitting something.
- Do not divide one thought into several isolated, quote-ready lines.
- Do not make every turn equally polished, equally brief, or equally witty.
- Allow contractions, unfinished starts, repeated words, small fillers, and changes of direction when they belong to the speaker. Natural speech may be slightly untidy, but do not manufacture errors as decoration.
- A character may partially answer, answer the implication instead of the literal question, or delay an answer, but the listener should be able to react to that choice.

### Exposition must be socially necessary

Information belongs in dialogue when someone in the scene needs it, disputes it, misunderstands it, or must make a decision with it. Filter explanations through the speaker's priorities and the listener's reactions.

Do not make one character recite headquarters systems, department roles, world facts, or game mechanics in outline order. Break information across actions and genuine questions. Let another character interrupt, test an assumption, notice a consequence, or pull the discussion toward what matters to them.

### Humor must react to something

Humor is strongest when it performs relationship work: easing tension, avoiding concern, testing familiarity, puncturing formality, or responding to an absurd detail. Do not add an interchangeable joke because the scene needs “a humor beat.” The setup must already exist in the conversation or shared history.

### Emotional turns must grow out of the exchange

Move from ordinary talk, work, or teasing toward sincerity by degrees. Let the character circle the vulnerable point, reveal it indirectly, or defer it into a promise or practical action. The sincere line should feel caused by what the two people have just said to each other.

### The spoken-aloud test

Before presenting dialogue, read it as a continuous exchange and ask:

- What caused each line?
- Is the speaker responding to the other person, or merely advancing the outline?
- Could another character say this unchanged?
- Did I turn a natural thought into several aphorisms?
- Is anyone stating their own character profile or the scene's theme?
- Does the rhythm vary naturally between reactions and developed thoughts?
- Would a person have a reason to say this aloud, here, to this listener?

Revise any line that fails these questions even if it is individually clever.

### Good IMC dialogue

- Names a concrete task or consequence.
- Carries conversational cause from one turn into the next.
- Lets profession shape metaphor and vocabulary.
- Uses pauses differently for different characters.
- Allows disagreement without making people cruel.
- Lets humor arise from identity rather than authorial one-liners.
- Keeps the player-character's dialogue flexible enough for self-insertion.
- Uses ordinary work as emotional subtext.
- Allows a thought to occupy the space it naturally needs.

### Avoid

- Everyone sounding equally witty.
- Alternating lines that each deliver one outline item.
- Uniformly clipped dialogue mistaken for character voice.
- Polished aphorisms where an ordinary response would be more human.
- Every scene ending in a dramatic confession.
- Lore speeches that do not affect a decision.
- Characters explaining their own personality.
- Characters announcing the theme or emotional conclusion before the conversation earns it.
- Generic “we need to save the world” stakes when a local consequence is available.
- Making serious characters secretly cowboys because the old project had a successful cowboy companion.

### Character-specific humor map

| Character | Humor source | What it should not become |
|---|---|---|
| Tristitia | Deliberate sass, controlled understatement, elegant superiority | Bubbly banter or Mae-like calm |
| IMC Elsie | Occasional dry professional observation, if used | Cowgirl speech, flirtatious gunslinger charm |

## 9. Emotional-turn rules for IMC

The Chasm of Hope pattern of banter-to-vulnerability should be adapted, not copied.

For IMC, use:

`work pressure → character-specific response → practical care → changed responsibility`

Possible emotional turns:

- An adventurer who complains about an expedition reveals they are afraid of disappointing the Company.
- Mae's calm breaks only when someone skips a meal or hides an injury.
- Liliana's over-explanation becomes a clear warning that saves a team.
- Tristitia's indirect praise becomes a promotion or a meaningful assignment.
- IMC Elsie's strict preparation is revealed as fear of sending someone into danger unprepared.
- The Commander chooses payroll, repairs, or recovery over a tempting shortcut.
- A successful expedition feels important because people at headquarters were waiting for its return.

The emotional reward should often be a changed routine: someone is invited to the table, trusted with a route, given a place to rest, allowed to speak first, or welcomed home.

### Darkness rule

When writing a dark beat, preserve the possibility of repair unless the story explicitly calls for irreversible loss. A serious scene may end with grief, but IMC should still remember the kitchen, the workshop, the route home, the waiting coworkers, or the next practical act of care.

### Relationship rule

Do not make affection depend only on romance. Attachment can be professional, familial, collegial, protective, rivalrous, or communal. The Company should feel emotionally rich even when no one is flirting.

## 10. Chapter 1 calibration

The current Chapter 1 draft is already pointed in the correct IMC direction: the player wakes in Eurydica, meets Tristitia, reaches a tavern, learns how monster processing works, and proposes a company.

### Scene 1: Opening

The scene's emotional job is not to create immediate epic danger. It establishes:

- The Commander as disoriented but observant.
- Tristitia as competent, controlled, and not needlessly cruel.
- Eurydica as dangerous but survivable.
- A first seed of trust: the Commander waits because he said he would.

Personalization guidance:

- Tristitia should give concise instructions and allow the Commander autonomy.
- Her line about the “monster” can carry dry sass, but should remain elegant and controlled.
- Her concern should appear through practical direction: where to rest, what to avoid, when she will return.

## 11. Agent operating procedure

When asked to create or revise an IMC story scene:

1. Identify the location, department, time, and immediate work problem.
2. List only the characters actually present.
3. Read each present character's current sheet. The character sheet outranks dialogue already written in the manuscript.
4. State what each character wants from the conversation, what they are avoiding, and what has just caused them to speak.
5. Decide what practical object, task, or consequence carries the subtext.
6. Build a turn map based on reactions and changing pressure, not a list of facts for each speaker to deliver.
7. Draft in conversational units. Preserve natural variation between short reactions and longer thoughts.
8. Keep humor character-specific and reactive to the current exchange.
9. Make the player-character's response options express different intentions while preserving his authored base voice.
10. Give the scene one emotional change, even if small, and let it alter the next action or responsibility.
11. End with a decision, task, invitation, consequence, or return.
12. Run the spoken-aloud test and contamination check below before delivering.

### Approval-first manuscript workflow

When the user asks to revise scene dialogue, do not edit the manuscript immediately.

1. Explain the intended changes and the specific dialogue problem they address.
2. Show the complete proposed dialogue in scene order, including enough action context to judge its rhythm.
3. Wait for explicit approval.
4. After approval, apply only the approved revision while preserving any newer user edits.

A request to review, brainstorm, or preview is not permission to modify the manuscript. Character sheets and the bible may be updated only when the user explicitly asks to change them.

### Contamination check

Before finalizing, ask:

- Did any character accidentally inherit Chasm of Hope Elsie's personality?
- Did IMC Elsie use cowboy language, western nicknames, or gunslinger charm?
- Did the setting become Fallout/New Vegas-like instead of Eurydica-like?
- Did the story become darker than the scene requires?
- Did the Company Commander personally solve a problem that should involve staff, adventurers, departments, or community members?
- Did the scene treat monsters as automatic villains instead of ecological threats or material sources?
- Did everyone use the same humor pattern?
- Did a placeholder character receive unsupported canon?
- Did the scene show how the Company or community is affected?

If any answer is yes, revise before delivery.

## 12. Compact prompt for future story work

Use this internal prompt when drafting:

> Write an IMC scene in Eurydica, a warm and slightly worn grounded-fantasy company-management world. The Company is both an institution and a household. Start from a concrete local problem or work task. Let profession shape dialogue. Preserve player choice through intent-based options. Give every named character a distinct voice, emotional armor, care language, and character-specific humor source. Use the Chasm of Hope craft principles of accumulated companionship, practical subtext, symbolic objects, recontextualizing reveals, and forward-moving emotional turns, but do not import Fallout/New Vegas setting, cowboy register, wasteland grimness, or Chasm Elsie's exact voice and gunslinger identity. IMC Elsie is warm and companionable but carries a former soldier's seriousness about expedition safety; she is not a cowgirl. Keep monsters ecological rather than automatically evil, keep magic open unless canonized, and make the scene change a relationship, responsibility, or community consequence before it ends.
>
> Write dialogue through conversational causality: every turn must be prompted by the previous words, action, silence, or shared situation and must pursue an immediate social intention. Do not convert outline points into alternating lines, split natural thoughts into polished aphorisms, or make characters state their own profiles. Vary the rhythm between short reactions and developed thoughts, make exposition necessary to a present decision, let humor react to the relationship, and let sincerity emerge from ordinary talk before it changes what happens next.
